# PROLEGOMENON
## Preamble: Establishing the Meaning of Value
### An Economic Field-State Comparative Lens for the Nexus Real-Value Standard

**This document should be read before the main Nexus proposal.**  
It does not replace the primary Nexus document. Its purpose is to establish the meaning of several terms—especially *value*—and to explain the comparative method used throughout the proposal.

---

## Why begin with a prolegomenon?

A *prolegomenon* is a preliminary discussion: something said beforehand because the ideas that follow cannot be understood properly until certain terms, assumptions, and methods have been made clear.

The word is uncommon, but the purpose is simple.

Before asking whether a monetary system can preserve value, we first have to ask:

**What do we mean by value?**

That question matters because the word *value* is used constantly in economics, finance, trade, politics, and ordinary life, but it does not always mean the same thing.

My father used to criticize me when I used words incorrectly. At the time, that could feel merely corrective. Over the years, however, the habit of stopping to ask what a word actually means became useful. The same curiosity extended to names, phrases, assumptions, and concepts people repeat without necessarily examining them.

Precision of language matters because a misunderstanding in a word can become a misunderstanding in an idea, and a misunderstanding in an idea can eventually become a flaw in an entire system.

Nexus begins by applying that discipline to the word **value**.

---

## One penny, four different values

Consider the smallest familiar denomination in the United States: the penny.

A penny has at least four distinct kinds of value:

1. **Face value** — the denomination stamped on it: one cent.
2. **Purchasing value** — what one cent can actually obtain in the marketplace.
3. **Material value** — the value of the physical material from which the coin is made.
4. **Scarcity or collector value** — what a particular coin may be worth because of rarity, age, condition, error, historical importance, or demand.

These four values do not necessarily move together.

A penny can retain a face value of one cent while its purchasing power declines. Its metal content can become worth more or less independently of what is stamped on the coin. If production ends, particular examples may become more desirable to collectors even though their usefulness in ordinary commerce remains negligible.

That simple coin reveals an important fact:

> **A monetary denomination can remain numerically unchanged while the economic reality represented by that denomination changes.**

This is the starting point for the Real-Value Standard.

## The monetary ruler

Civilization depends on standards of measurement.

A centimeter does not become shorter because the economy enters a recession.  
A kilogram does not lose mass because a government runs a deficit.  
A second does not become longer because prices rise.

We establish standards of length, mass, and time precisely because measurement becomes unreliable if the measuring instrument itself changes.

Money is different.

The monetary unit keeps its name while its purchasing capability can change.

A dollar remains called a dollar.  
A cent remains called a cent.  
Yet what those units command in goods, services, labor, shelter, energy, transportation, and other necessities can change substantially over time.

This raises a fundamental question:

> **Why do we accept a monetary ruler whose numerical markings remain fixed while the economic length of the ruler changes?**

Nexus does not begin by assuming that all prices should be frozen. Prices must be able to change. Scarcity changes. Technology changes. Productivity changes. Preferences change. A poor harvest should not be represented as though grain remains equally abundant. A breakthrough in manufacturing may legitimately make a product cheaper.

The question is different:

> **Can changes in the underlying monetary measuring unit be distinguished from legitimate changes in the things being measured?**

That is the purpose of the Real-Value Standard.

## Value instability is older than modern economics

The instability of value is probably as old as exchange itself.

Before coinage, before paper currency, before central banks, people traded one useful thing for another. If grain became scarce after a failed harvest, more grain might be demanded in exchange for a goat, a tool, or a day's labor. If metal became abundant, its exchange relationship to other goods could change.

So **value instability begins with exchange**.

Monetary inflation, in the stricter sense, becomes possible when societies create common units of account and exchange. Once many goods are measured against a shared monetary reference, that reference itself can be altered, diluted, expanded, debased, or otherwise changed.

Ancient coinage provides an obvious example. A coin could retain its familiar name and legal denomination while containing progressively less precious metal. Commerce could continue because people still needed a common medium of exchange, taxes still had to be paid, wages still had to be received, and the monetary network still functioned.

This leads to an important distinction:

> **Continued use of a currency demonstrates its acceptance and necessity. It does not prove preservation of its value.**

A monetary system can function as a medium of exchange while becoming progressively less reliable as a store of purchasing capability.

---

## Value erosion and value extraction

Not every rise in prices is evidence of wrongdoing.

A drought can destroy crops.  
A war can destroy productive capacity.  
An energy shortage can raise costs throughout an economy.  
Demand can rise faster than supply.

These are forms of **value erosion** or price pressure that can occur without anyone intentionally taking value from someone else.

But a different category exists when an institution deliberately alters the monetary system in a way that transfers purchasing capability away from existing holders.

That can be described as **value extraction**.

The distinction matters.

Nexus is not built on the claim that every episode of inflation has a single cause, or that every increase in price is theft. It is built on the proposition that the loss of purchasing capability is real, measurable, consequential, and too important to be treated merely as an unavoidable background condition.

The central question is therefore not simply:

**Why do prices rise?**

It is:

> **Why should the monetary unit itself be permitted to lose purchasing capability without a system designed to detect, measure, counterbalance, and prevent that loss from compounding across generations?**

## The Economic Field-State Comparative Lens

The method proposed here can be understood as an **Economic Field-State Comparative Lens**.

The phrase borrows a way of thinking from comparative field analysis: instead of examining one isolated number in one isolated moment, examine the entire configuration of conditions surrounding that number.

In economic terms, a *field state* may include:

- the monetary unit,
- purchasing capability,
- public and private debt,
- productive output,
- inflation or deflation,
- population,
- scarcity,
- wages,
- taxation,
- public obligations,
- interest,
- incentives,
- institutional rules,
- and the passage of time.

The purpose is not merely to compare "$1 then" with "$1 now."

It is to compare the **whole economic condition** in which that dollar exists.

---

## The slide-rule method

One way to visualize the Economic Field-State Comparative Lens is as a slide rule laid across history.

Place a large modern liability—such as tens of trillions of dollars in accumulated public debt—at the present end of the scale.

Now slide the point of recognition backward.

What if society had recognized the future liability:

- 20 years earlier?
- 100 years earlier?
- at the beginning of the Federal Reserve era?
- at the founding of the United States?
- during the age of classical coinage?
- at the earliest stages of organized trade?

This is not an attempt to rewrite history. It is a **counterfactual analytical instrument**.

At each point on the slide rule, ask:

> **How small would a recurring contribution from newly created real value have needed to be, if preserved and compounded over time, to prevent or retire the later liability?**

The further back the recognition point moves, the greater the role of time and compounding.

A burden that appears nearly impossible when attacked only in the present may look radically different when distributed across a much longer period of genuine value creation.

This leads to a broader principle:

> **The earlier a civilization reserves a small portion of newly created value for long-term obligations, the less severe the burden imposed on any one generation.**

But repayment alone is insufficient.

If the mechanism that creates the liability remains unchanged, a debt-retirement fund merely becomes another way to refill a bucket with a hole in it.

Therefore the slide-rule method has to be run in both directions.

Looking backward asks:

**What contribution would have prevented the accumulation?**

Looking forward asks:

**What rules and incentives are required to prevent the same accumulation from returning?**

---

## Retirement, prevention, and incentive

A durable system requires at least three functions.

### 1. Retirement

A defined portion of genuine new value creation can be directed toward reducing inherited obligations.

### 2. Prevention

The mechanisms that continually recreate unsustainable liabilities must be identified and constrained.

### 3. Incentive

Individuals, businesses, communities, and governments should benefit from creating additional real value without creating an equivalent or greater future liability.

A system based only on prohibition invites circumvention.

A system that aligns incentives with long-term value preservation has a better chance of becoming self-reinforcing.

This is why Nexus treats incentives as a structural component rather than an afterthought.

## What Nexus is—and is not—trying to stabilize

Nexus is not attempting to make every kind of value identical.

It is not trying to force:

- face value,
- purchasing value,
- material value,
- and collector value

to become the same thing.

Nor is it trying to prevent legitimate market prices from changing.

The target is more specific:

> **Nexus is concerned with preserving real purchasing capability as a measurable economic reference.**

That requires a benchmark broader than a single commodity.

Gold can change in value.  
Oil can change in value.  
Housing can change in value.  
Food can change in value.  
Labor can change in value.

A Real-Value Standard would therefore need to observe a sufficiently broad field of essential and productive economic activity so that changes in one category do not masquerade as changes in the entire monetary system.

The goal is not a frozen economy.

The goal is a more stable measuring instrument.

---

## A Real-Value Cent

The penny can serve as a useful conceptual specimen.

Imagine a hypothetical **Real-Value Cent**.

An ordinary cent has a fixed nominal designation:

**1 cent**

But its purchasing power changes.

A Real-Value Cent would instead represent a defined fraction of a continuously measured purchasing-value benchmark.

The question then becomes:

> **What would one cent have to mean for one cent to remain one cent in real economic terms?**

That is not yet an implementation formula. It is a calibration question.

And calibration questions are important because they force us to distinguish the label printed on the unit from the economic capability represented by the unit.

## Why this must be read first

The main Nexus proposal presents gates, tests, safeguards, and mechanisms intended to evaluate whether a Real-Value Standard could function without creating greater instability elsewhere.

But those mechanisms make little sense if the reader assumes that *value* has only one meaning.

The penny demonstrates that it does not.

The slide-rule method demonstrates that time changes the scale of economic obligations.

The Economic Field-State Comparative Lens demonstrates that a monetary unit cannot be understood in isolation from the wider system in which it operates.

So before evaluating Nexus, keep three questions in view:

1. **What form of value is being discussed?**
2. **Is the observed change coming from the thing being priced, or from the monetary measuring unit?**
3. **What does the same problem look like when viewed from another economic field state in time?**

Those questions are the context for everything that follows.

---

## The central proposition

The Real-Value Standard begins with a simple observation:

> **A civilization that insists on stable standards for length, mass, and time should at least investigate whether purchasing value can be measured—and protected—with comparable seriousness.**

Nexus is an attempt to conduct that investigation.

It does not assume the answer in advance.

It asks that the system be made explicit, measurable, falsifiable, stress-tested, and resistant to the reappearance of the very conditions it is intended to solve.

That is why this prolegomenon comes first.

Before redesigning money, we must first understand what we mean when we say **value**.
